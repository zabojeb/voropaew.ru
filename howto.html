HOW TO ANTI-DESIGN THINGS

A design revolution happens when established aesthetics become boring and provoke a rebellious response. This is how brutalism, web brutalism, neo-brutalism, and anti-design emerged — movements that deliberately violate the canons of "beautiful" design.

Brutalism

HONEST

Brutalism originated in 1950s architecture as "béton brut" — raw concrete, and that's where its name comes from. The first brutalist buildings were monolithic concrete blocks without finishing — rough, simple, functional. The idea was honesty of materials: structures demonstrated their structure and material directly, without embellishment.

In web design, brutalism manifested as an attempt to give sites a "raw," unpolished look, deliberately opposing itself to glossy web design. Supporters of the style value it for its boldness and honesty — they want the Internet to look truthful, "as is," without gloss and wrappers.

The brutalist aesthetic on screen is a simplified, sometimes even crude appearance of pages. People often mention this image: "imagine a bare HTML site with blue links and monospaced black text on a white background." This is exactly what early 90s sites looked like.

Web Brutalism

REBELLION

Emerging around the mid-2010s, web brutalism took shape as a protest against overly smooth and template web design. Designer Pascal Deville launched BrutalistWebsites.com in 2014, where he collected examples of such sites.

The main task of a brutal site is to make the interface as simple and understandable as possible, "without visual excesses: no rounding, no variegation, no unnecessary design."

Don't confuse web brutalism with classical minimalism. Although both styles avoid embellishment, brutalism has a different vibe. Distinctive features look deliberately provocative:

  • Sharp, "toxic" colors (screaming blue, red, acid green) in large blocks and headings
  • Pure "raw" background — most often white or black, without gradients
  • Absence of complex graphics and processed images
  • Widespread use of one simple font, often monospaced
  • Complete absence of animation and special effects
  • Intentional violations in the grid and content hierarchy

L'Internet Brut

The first type — "raw internet" — is closest to the ideas of architectural brutalism. Sites of this type almost completely shed decorative ballast: white background, blue links, black text, minimum CSS. Example? Wikipedia.

L'Internet Fou

The second type — "crazy internet" — is the most controversial manifestation. Such sites are deliberately complicated and chaotic: bright neon colors, rough shapes, strange cursors, meaningless animations, complete absence of the usual hierarchy.

Neo-Brutalism

HYBRID

If web brutalism is a rebellion against excessive aestheticization, then neo-brutalism can be called an attempt to reconcile the rebellious spirit with modern design standards. This style emerged in the 2020s as a kind of compromise.

Essentially, neo-brutalism is a hybrid of minimalism and brutalism. It takes the best from minimalism (clean grid, orderliness, functionality) and mixes it with what attracted in brutalism (freedom of expression, audacity, brightness).

Key features: Large, bold typography. High contrast and clear boundaries. Bright "conflicting" colors. Simplicity of forms and deliberate imperfection. Despite the riot of color, neo-brutalist design often uses elements in their original, "cut-down" form.

Anti-Design

CHAOS

Anti-design as a concept arose not in the digital age — its roots go back to the 1960s. In Italy, a group of young designers rebelled against the dominant modernism that asserted "form follows function." These radicals believed design had become too serious, utilitarian, and connected to consumer culture.

The philosophy of anti-design originally consisted of denying functionalism for the sake of artistic statement. To destroy habitual ideas about an object, anti-designers used kitsch, absurd forms, distorted proportions, screaming colors.

In web design, anti-design manifested as an extension of extreme web brutalism. When some designers said "let's make sites simpler," others went further and said "let's deliberately make it bad." The result was a paradox: creating design that contradicts generally accepted design norms.

Important: anti-design on the web is not just an ugly interface, but a conscious violation of UX canons with an artistic or social idea.

What distinguishes anti-design?

  • Complete ignorance of user habit. An anti-design site breaks perception patterns: familiar navigation may be absent, the logic of element placement is chaotic
  • Deliberately bad aesthetics. Here "ugliness" is cultivated as a style: the designer specifically chooses tasteless color combinations
  • Deliberate complication of interaction. While good UX seeks to simplify, anti-design does the opposite: inserts obstacles to touch emotions
  • Irony and provocation. Almost always anti-design projects contain a share of humor. This is design with a wink

The 5 Key Principles

1. Break rules consciously. Forget about grids, guidelines and order — chaos is your best friend. Don't be afraid to do it "wrong" — in anti-design there's no wrong except boredom.

2. Aestheticize ugliness. Turn the ugly into style: combine the most toxic colors, mix fonts of all calibers, use outdated effects. What an ordinary designer would consider a nightmare is a find for you.

3. Put emotion above convenience. The goal of anti-design is to evoke a reaction, be it laughter, surprise, or even indignation. Shock is your tool.

4. Ironize and play. Anti-design without humor is dead. Put a dose of self-irony into your crazy decisions. Let knowledgeable people read: you're not an incompetent dilettante, but a cunning provocateur.

5. Know the measure of rebellion. Not every project can withstand such an approach. If you're making a site not for creative experiment, but for a real audience — think twice.

Perception Function F

F(x)

Design is a point in a huge space of options. We can pretend the brain evaluates each point with a rough function F — a mix of usability, novelty, emotion, clarity and irony.

F = wu·usability + wn·novelty + we·emotion + wc·clarity + wi·irony/tension

Classic product design pushes F by letting usability and clarity dominate. Anti-design tweaks the weights: it willingly burns a part of usability to boost novelty, emotion and tension.

5 Unwritten Rules

DO NOT KILL IT

Even the loudest anti-design lives inside a frame. Cross these lines and the whole thing stops being a deliberate statement and collapses into pure noise.

  1. Keep the core readable. The main message, call to action or navigation has to survive the chaos. If the user literally cannot find what the thing is about, there is no subversion — only entropy.
  2. Make chaos consistent. Choose one type of “wrongness” and repeat it: same kind of broken grid, same kind of ugly color, same kind of type abuse. Random, unstyled mistakes feel like incompetence, not a stance.
  3. Keep a macro-structure. Sections, flows, beginnings and endings must still exist. The user can feel lost inside a section, but they should not feel lost in the entire universe. We SHOULD break the banner blindness, but we MUST not break the flow, that's the point.
  4. Have a concept, not just ugliness. "We made it ugly because lol" dies fast. There has to be a story: parodying perfect UX, quoting early web, echoing a subculture or critiquing something outside the screen, or simply showing your vision, the story you want to tell.
  5. Respect time and bodies. This rule mostly comes from brutalism. Do not make interactions physically impossible or harmful. No seizure-level flickering, no unreadable microcopy for critical actions, no puzzles where a simple click would do.

Examples

Web Brutalism:

BrutalistWebsites.com — an entire collection of brutalist sites. The motto says: "Brutalism can be seen as a reaction by a younger generation to the lightness and optimism of today's web design."

Wikipedia — surprisingly, Wikipedia can be called a brutalist site. Its interface is extremely simple and functional: the main thing is content, design is secondary.

Craigslist — one of the most famous examples of old web design, cited as a prototype of web brutalism. Its appearance has hardly changed since the 1990s.

Anti-Design:

Bloomberg Businessweek Design 2016 — the first virally famous anti-design project. The creators deliberately made it chaotic: moving objects, distracting animation, confusing inscriptions. Users were shocked but the site attracted massive attention.

Weather Is Happening — complete absurdity: the page shimmers with acid colors, text is written in mixed case, meaningless words like "WAIT" and "WAT" pop up everywhere.

Yale School of Art (old site) — famous for its deliberately awkward design. Students designed it as anti-art: unaligned text blocks, chaotic colors, strange backgrounds.

HOW TO ANTI-DESIGN THINGS is not a call to make all sites ugly, but an invitation to look at design from a different angle.

Brutalism, web brutalism, neo-brutalism and anti-design arose as a reaction to satiety with the ideal of beauty and convenience. They return to creativity a share of risk, experiment and living protest spirit.

The main thing these movements remind us: design is not dogma, but a game of imagination. Sometimes, to find something new, you need to destroy the old (even jokingly).

As the radicals of the 60s said, "everything correct sooner or later becomes boring." And that means there will always be room for new brutalists and anti-designers who will remind us: rules exist to be broken.
